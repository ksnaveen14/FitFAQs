## Q: How much protein do I need to build muscle?
Research consistently shows that 1.6–2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day
is the optimal range for muscle protein synthesis in resistance-trained individuals. Going higher
(up to 3.0 g/kg) has not been shown to provide additional muscle-building benefit, though it
is not harmful. Protein quality matters: prioritize complete proteins containing all essential
amino acids (meat, eggs, dairy, soy).

## Q: Is creatine safe and effective?
Creatine monohydrate is one of the most well-researched supplements in sports nutrition.
It is both safe and effective for improving strength, power output, and muscle mass.
Dose: 3–5 grams daily. No loading phase required. Benefits appear within 2–4 weeks.
It works by increasing phosphocreatine stores in muscle, which fuels ATP regeneration
during high-intensity efforts lasting 1–10 seconds.

## Q: Should I train fasted?
Fasted training (typically morning sessions before eating) does not significantly enhance
fat loss compared to fed training when total calories are matched over the day. Performance
may suffer during fasted sessions for some individuals, particularly in strength sports.
If you train fasted and feel strong, it is fine. If performance drops, eat a small pre-workout
snack (banana, protein shake).

## Q: What is intermittent fasting and does it help with fat loss?
Intermittent fasting (IF) is an eating pattern that cycles between periods of fasting and eating.
Common protocols: 16:8 (fast 16 hours, eat in 8-hour window), 5:2 (eat normally 5 days,
restrict calories 2 days). IF is a tool for controlling caloric intake, not a metabolic magic trick.
It works for fat loss only if it helps you sustain a caloric deficit. It is not superior to
traditional caloric restriction when protein intake and calories are equated.

## Q: Can I gain muscle and lose fat at the same time?
Body recomposition — simultaneous muscle gain and fat loss — is possible, but predominantly
for three groups: complete beginners, detrained individuals returning to exercise, and people
with high body fat percentage. For trained individuals at relatively low body fat, the most
efficient approach is alternating focused bulking and cutting phases.

## Q: How important is sleep for fitness results?
Sleep is arguably the most underrated factor in fitness. During slow-wave (deep) sleep, growth
hormone is released at its highest levels. Sleep deprivation reduces testosterone, elevates
cortisol, impairs muscle protein synthesis, and reduces glycogen replenishment. Aim for
7–9 hours per night. Poor sleep can negate the benefits of good training and nutrition.

## Q: What supplements are actually worth taking?
Based on evidence quality:

Tier 1 (Strong evidence, safe):
- Creatine monohydrate: Strength and power
- Caffeine: Endurance and focus
- Protein powder: Convenient dietary protein

Tier 2 (Moderate evidence):
- Beta-alanine: Muscular endurance (causes tingling harmlessly)
- Vitamin D3: Important if deficient (most people are)
- Omega-3 fatty acids: Anti-inflammatory, heart health

Tier 3 (Weak evidence or overhyped):
- BCAAs (unnecessary if protein intake is adequate)
- Pre-workout blends (just caffeine + marketing)
- Fat burners (largely ineffective and sometimes dangerous)

## Q: How do I break a weight-loss plateau?
Plateaus occur because your body adapts — your TDEE decreases as you lose weight.
Solutions:
1. Recalculate TDEE based on current bodyweight and adjust calories downward
2. Increase NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis): walk more, take stairs
3. Add or increase cardio volume
4. Implement a diet break: eat at maintenance for 1–2 weeks to restore leptin levels
5. Track food more accurately — portion creep is common

## Q: Are carbohydrates bad for you?
No. Carbohydrates are not inherently fattening. Fat gain results from a caloric surplus,
not from carbohydrates specifically. Carbs are the primary fuel for brain function and
high-intensity exercise. The quality of carbohydrates matters: whole grains, fruits, and
vegetables provide fiber and micronutrients. Refined sugars and processed carbs should
be minimized because they are calorie-dense and nutrient-poor.

## Q: How long does it take to see results from strength training?
Beginners typically notice strength gains within 2–3 weeks due to neuromuscular adaptations
(your nervous system learns to recruit muscle fibers more efficiently). Visible muscle growth
(hypertrophy) begins appearing at 6–8 weeks with consistent training and adequate nutrition.
Significant physique changes typically become apparent to others at 3–6 months.
Patience and consistency are the two most critical success factors.
